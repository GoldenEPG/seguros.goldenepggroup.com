What is business liability insurance?

Business liability insurance can help protect a business from certain third-party claims. A common policy is Commercial General Liability (CGL), which may cover eligible bodily injury, property damage, personal or advertising injury and certain completed operations claims. Actual coverage depends on the issued policy, limits, exclusions and endorsements.

Texas reference: the Texas Department of Insurance explains commercial general liability coverage for business owners.

Coverage Boundaries

What general liability is not designed to replace

General liability is important, but it is only one part of a commercial insurance program. Separate coverage may be needed for vehicles, professional services, employee injuries, property, tools or other specialized exposures.

Business Vehicles

Vehicle liability is generally handled through the appropriate commercial auto policy rather than a general liability policy.

Review Car Insurance →

Professional Errors

Claims involving professional advice, errors or omissions may require professional liability or errors and omissions coverage.

Employee Injuries

Work-related employee injury exposures should be reviewed separately from general liability, including workers' compensation considerations.

Your Property & Tools

Damage to your own building, inventory, equipment or tools may require commercial property, inland marine or a Business Owner's Policy, depending on the risk.

Business Liability vs Other Coverage

General liability does not replace every type of business insurance.

A strong commercial insurance program often combines policies. The right mix depends on your operations, vehicles, employees, property, contracts and professional services.

Quote Checklist

What to prepare before requesting business liability insurance

Clear underwriting information helps an agent and carrier understand the real risk. For contractors and service businesses, the exact work performed matters more than a broad business label.

1Exact operations: describe the work you actually perform, not only the company name or NAICS label.
2Annual sales and payroll: estimated gross receipts and payroll for the policy period.
3Employees and subcontractors: how many people work for you and what subcontracted work is used.
4Job locations: office-only, customer premises, construction sites, retail locations or other work sites.
5Contract requirements: requested limits, additional insured wording, waiver requests or COI instructions.
6Prior claims: loss history and any known circumstances the carrier asks about.
Why this matters: two businesses with the same broad label can have very different underwriting exposure. Accurate operations help reduce mismatches between the quote and the work being performed.

Certificates of Insurance

Need a Certificate of Insurance (COI)?

A Certificate of Insurance (COI) is used as evidence that insurance coverage exists. For risks located in Texas, certificate forms must comply with Texas requirements. A COI cannot create, expand or change coverage beyond the actual policy and endorsements. Additional insured status, waiver requests and other certificate information must be supported by the policy and applicable endorsements.

Review Insurance Requirements

We can review the insurance requirements provided to you and identify coverage items that may need attention. We provide insurance guidance, not legal advice, and cannot certify contract terms that are not supported by the policy.

Match the Policy

Limits, additional insured status, waiver requests and other endorsements must be supported by the actual policy and applicable carrier-approved endorsements.

Provide Evidence

Once coverage is in force, an appropriate Texas-compliant certificate may be used to show evidence of coverage. The certificate itself does not modify the policy.

Business Liability FAQ

Questions Houston business owners frequently ask

Is business liability insurance the same as general liability insurance?

“Business liability insurance” is a broad phrase people often use when searching for liability protection for a business. Commercial general liability is one of the most common policies used to address third-party bodily injury, property damage, and certain personal and advertising injury claims.

What does general liability insurance cover for a Texas business?

Depending on the policy, general liability can help protect against covered third-party claims involving bodily injury, property damage, and personal or advertising injury. Products/completed operations protection may also apply subject to the policy.

Does business liability insurance cover my commercial vehicle?

General liability is not a substitute for commercial auto insurance. Businesses using vehicles for work should review the appropriate commercial auto or car insurance coverage separately.

Can I get a Certificate of Insurance for a client or general contractor?

If the policy is in force, a certificate may be used as evidence of available insurance. For a risk located in Texas, the certificate form must meet Texas requirements. Requested limits, additional insured status, waiver of subrogation or other endorsements must be supported by the actual policy and carrier approval; a certificate cannot create coverage that the policy does not provide.

How much does business liability insurance cost?

Pricing depends on factors such as business type, operations, location, revenue, payroll, employees, subcontractors, claims history, coverage limits and underwriting. The most reliable way to determine cost is to quote the actual business.

Is general liability insurance required for every business in Texas?

There is not one general liability requirement that applies to every Texas business. However, specific industries, licenses, contracts, landlords, clients or project owners may require liability insurance or particular limits. A licensed agent can help review the insurance requirement that applies to your business; legal or licensing questions should be confirmed with the appropriate authority.
